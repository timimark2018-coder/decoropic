# QC & Consolidation from China: The Project Inspection Checklist

On a project material order, quality control is not a formality at the end — it's the step that decides whether your finishes arrive right or your fit-out slips. Consolidation is the step that decides whether you pay for one shipment or five. Handled together, they are where a China-sourced package earns its cost advantage. Here's the working checklist.

## Why QC comes before payment, not after

The economics are simple: catching a wrong finish or dimension **at the factory** costs a re-make; catching it **at your site** costs the project. That's why experienced buyers hold the balance payment to a QC gate and require inspection reports before goods leave China.

## The inspection checklist

**Before production**
- Approved control samples for every finish (color, tone, sheen, texture).
- A pre-production sample for key casegoods/joinery.
- A written spec matrix each item is checked against.

**In-line (during production)**
- Early-run check on the first batch to catch systemic issues before the whole order is made.
- Verify materials and hardware match the approved sample, not a substitute.

**Pre-shipment (before it leaves the factory)**
- Random-sample inspection against the approved samples and spec.
- Dimensions, finish, function (drawers, hinges, switches), and quantity.
- Packaging and labelling suitable for sea freight and your market.
- **Photo/video report** documenting findings.

**Payment control**
- Release the balance only after the pre-shipment report is accepted.

## Consolidation: one project, one shipment

Most projects draw from several factories. Consolidation brings them into coordinated FCL/LCL loads:

- **Lower freight** per unit versus multiple small shipments.
- **One set of clearance documents** for your broker.
- **One arrival** to schedule your installation crews around.
- **A single packing manifest** you can reconcile against your spec.

Consolidation also creates a natural final-check point: as goods arrive at the consolidation warehouse, quantities and cartons are verified against the manifest before loading.

## Documentation that travels with the goods

- Commercial invoice and detailed packing list.
- Certificate/Declaration of Origin where your market's duty treatment requires it.
- Any product certifications your destination mandates (arranged where required).
- Fumigation/treatment certificates for timber and packaging where applicable.

## The four inspection points, and what each one is for

**1. Pre-production — the sample gate.** Before anything is made in volume, approve a physical sample for every finish and a pre-production sample for anything bespoke. Photographs are not approval; screen colour is not colour. This is the cheapest point at which to be difficult.

**2. During production (in-line).** Typically at 20–50% completion, while errors can still be corrected in the same run. This is the inspection buyers most often skip and most often regret, because it is the last point at which a wrong finish is a correction rather than a re-make.

**3. Pre-shipment — before anything is loaded.** Against the approved sample and the packing list. A pre-shipment inspection that happens after loading is a formality, not a control.

**4. Container loading (loading supervision).** Confirms that what passed inspection is what actually goes into the box, in the condition and quantity agreed, correctly braced.

Third-party inspection from an SGS or Bureau Veritas-grade firm runs **$200–400 per inspector-day**. On a project shipment that is a rounding error against the cost of one container of wrong goods.

## What a useful inspection report contains

Ask for these explicitly, in the purchase order, before anyone books a flight:

- Photographs of the goods **against the approved sample**, not in isolation
- Quantity verification line by line against the packing list
- Carton, crate and pallet condition, with photographs of any damage
- Dimensional checks on the lines where dimension matters — slab calibration, door leaves, joinery
- Function checks on anything mechanical or electrical: drawers, hinges, mixers, drivers, switches
- Copies of certificates the specification requires — the certificate itself, not a claim that one exists
- Photographs of the loaded container before the doors close

## When something fails

Agree the process before it happens, because the moment goods fail is the worst time to negotiate one. A workable clause covers: who decides pass or fail, what the tolerance is for minor cosmetic defects, whether partial shipment is permitted while a failed line is re-made, who pays for re-inspection, and how a re-make affects the shipping date.

The practical lever is payment terms. A balance payable after successful pre-shipment inspection concentrates attention in a way that no email chain does.

## Consolidation: the mechanics

Consolidation means goods from several factories are collected into one warehouse, checked, re-palletised where needed, and loaded into a single container under one export entry. The saving is typically **$1,500–3,000** per project in duplicated logistics overhead — one customs entry instead of five, one set of port charges, one inland delivery at the far end.

The scheduling saving is often worth more than the money: one arrival date to plan trades around, instead of five deliveries landing in an order nobody controls.

## Volume planning

A 20ft container takes roughly **18–22 m³** packed; a 40ft takes **33–38 m³**; a 40ft high-cube adds about **0.3 m** of internal height, which matters once you are stacking wardrobes, door leaves or crated lighting. Always plan on **packed** volume from the factory rather than product dimensions — crating and pallets routinely add 15–25% of bulk, which is what turns a three-container job into a four-container one.

Sea freight from South China ran **$1,800–3,200 per 40ft** through 2026 and moves with the market; re-confirm quotes older than four weeks. Direct sailings run **28–35 days**; production is **7–15 days** for standard items and **20–40 days** for bespoke or large-format; from confirmed order to material on site, plan **10–12 weeks**.

## Two category-specific checks worth writing in

For tile, order a **5–8% breakage and cutting allowance** and require a **single calibrated batch** — a **±0.5 mm** variation between batches shows as uneven joints on a large floor and cannot be tiled out. For anything upholstered or panel-based, check that the fire and emission certificates named in the specification are the ones actually supplied, and that they cover the material used rather than a similar product from the same factory.
## Documents that have to match

A surprising share of clearance delays are not quality problems at all — they are paperwork mismatches. Before the container sails, check that the commercial invoice, packing list, bill of lading and any certificate of origin describe the **same goods in the same words**, with consistent quantities, weights and values.

Specifically: descriptions should match across all documents rather than being paraphrased on each; carton and pallet counts should reconcile between the packing list and the bill of lading; gross and net weights should be consistent; and any preferential origin claim needs the evidence attached, not promised.

Your customs broker at the destination should see draft documents **before** shipment, not on arrival. A correction made while the goods are still in the warehouse costs an email; the same correction made at the port costs storage.
