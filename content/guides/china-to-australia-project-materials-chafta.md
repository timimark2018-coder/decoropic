# Importing Project Materials from China to Australia — A Developer's Guide (ChAFTA)

Australia is one of the more tariff-friendly destinations for materials made in China, thanks to the China–Australia Free Trade Agreement (ChAFTA). For developers and builders delivering hotels, apartment buildings and villas, that duty advantage — combined with project-based sourcing — can make a real difference to landed cost. Here's how to use it well.

## The ChAFTA advantage — used correctly

Under ChAFTA, many goods of Chinese origin are eligible for preferential (often free) customs duty when imported into Australia, provided origin requirements are met and the correct documentation (such as a Certificate of Origin or a Declaration of Origin) accompanies the shipment. The preference is real, but it is **conditional**: eligibility and paperwork depend on the specific tariff classification and origin rules. Treat any duty figure as *reported*, and confirm your product's classification, current rate, and documentation requirements with a licensed customs broker before you order. Decoropic can flag commonly reported figures with sources but does not act as your customs agent.

## Australian compliance is the other half

Duty is only one gate. Australian projects also face product-compliance expectations — for example, electrical equipment approvals, plumbing/WaterMark for certain sanitaryware and tapware, glass and structural standards, and labelling. Build compliance into the specification from the start; a great price on a fitting that can't be certified in Australia is not a saving. Confirm which of your items require certification and plan the evidence early.

## Why a project material package still wins

Even with a duty advantage, buying category by category from unrelated factories reintroduces the old problems: mismatched finishes, staggered lead times, and multiple arrivals. A coordinated **material package** — one spec, one QC standard, one consolidated shipment — keeps the ChAFTA saving from being eaten by coordination cost.

## Consolidation and freight

Consolidating multiple factories into coordinated FCL/LCL loads to Australian ports (Sydney, Melbourne, Brisbane, Fremantle) reduces per-unit freight and simplifies both your ChAFTA documentation and your quarantine/biosecurity paperwork — timber and packaging in particular attract biosecurity attention, so declare and treat correctly.

## QC before it ships

Pre-shipment inspection against approved samples, with photo reports, is the safeguard that keeps a finish or dimension problem from becoming a site problem an ocean away. Hold the balance payment to the QC gate.

## Where the ChAFTA advantage actually shows up

The agreement's value is not evenly spread. It matters most where the alternative sources are expensive and the tariff line would otherwise bite: ceramic tile and stone, sanitaryware and brassware, contract and residential furniture, decorative and architectural lighting, and door and window hardware. These are exactly the categories that make up the bulk of a fit-out package by value.

It matters less on items where duty was already low or where freight and compliance dominate the landed cost — some raw commodities, and anything so bulky that the container cost outweighs the tariff saving.

The practical read: for an Australian developer, a China-sourced fit-out package is usually competitive on the finishes and furniture layer, which is where most of the specification budget sits anyway.

## Claiming preferential treatment — the paperwork that decides it

Preferential rates are not automatic. They are claimed, and a claim that is not properly evidenced simply gets assessed at the standard rate. What you need in place before the container sails:

- A **certificate of origin** or an approved declaration of origin from the exporter, matching the goods actually shipped
- Consistency across the commercial invoice, packing list and bill of lading — a description mismatch is one of the more common reasons a claim gets queried
- The correct tariff classification for each line, agreed with your customs broker in advance rather than argued at the border
- Evidence retained for the period your customs authority requires, in case of post-clearance audit

Get the classification wrong and the correction lands after the goods have been sold or installed, which is the expensive way to find out.

## The comparison that is genuinely useful

Australian buyers regularly ask how their position compares with Europe or the United States. The honest answer is that trade measures on some Chinese-origin materials — ceramic tile in particular — are materially heavier into the EU and US than into Australia, which is why a package that makes no commercial sense shipped to Rotterdam can work very well shipped to Fremantle. We cover how those measures work, and how to verify your own exposure, in the [tariff verification guide](/guides/china-materials-tariffs-eu-us-how-to-verify).

*Rates and trade measures change, are set by the destination authority, and depend on your exact classification and origin evidence. Treat anything you read — here or elsewhere — as a planning estimate and confirm your position with a licensed Australian customs broker before you order. Figures reflect August 2026.*

## Compliance is a separate gate from tariffs

Duty is the question buyers ask about; compliance is the one that stops containers. Australian projects carry expectations around electrical approval and plug configuration for anything that draws power, glazing and safety glass requirements, water-efficiency and plumbing approval for sanitaryware and tapware, and formaldehyde emission limits on engineered panel products.

Decide early, per category, **who** is responsible for certification — the factory, an appointed test house, or your own consultant — and write it into the purchase specification. A tap that cannot be legally installed is not cheaper than a compliant one, regardless of the FOB price.

## Volume, freight and timing for an Australian programme

A 20ft container holds roughly **18–22 m³** packed; a 40ft holds **33–38 m³**; a 40ft high-cube adds about **0.3 m** of height, useful for tall joinery, door leaves and crated lighting. Sea freight from South China ran **$1,800–3,200 per 40ft** through 2026 and moves with the market — re-confirm any quote older than four weeks.

On timing: standard items produce in **7–15 days**, bespoke and large-format slab in **20–40 days**, and sea transit runs **28–35 days**. From confirmed order to material on site, plan **10–12 weeks**. Consolidating multiple factories into a single shipment typically saves **$1,500–3,000** in duplicated logistics overhead, and — just as valuable on a programme — gives you one arrival date to plan trades around instead of five.

For tile specifically, budget a **5–8% breakage and cutting allowance**, and insist that large-format slab comes from a single calibrated batch: a **±0.5 mm** variation between batches is enough to make joints visibly uneven once laid.
