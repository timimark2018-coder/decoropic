# Kitchen Worktops in Ghana: Quartz, Sintered Stone, Granite and the Local vs Import Question

The worktop takes more abuse than any other surface in a Ghanaian kitchen: hot pots straight off the stove, palm oil and tomato stains, heavy chopping and constant wiping. It is also one of the most visible finishes. This guide compares the worktop materials available for Ghana — granite, engineered quartz, sintered stone, solid surface and laminate — on heat, stain and scratch resistance, maintenance and installation, and explains when a worktop should be bought and cut locally and when it should ship with an imported kitchen.

## How Ghanaian cooking changes the brief

Many homes cook with high heat, oil-heavy dishes and sometimes charcoal or gas on an outdoor or back kitchen. A worktop specification should assume:

- Hot pans placed directly on the surface, at least occasionally
- Oil, pepper and tomato-based stains left for hours
- Frequent washing with strong detergents or bleach
- Heavy items (mortar and pestle, large pots) set down hard
- High ambient humidity, so substrates under laminate must be moisture-resistant

## Granite: the local default

Granite remains the most common upgrade worktop in Ghana because slabs are stocked and cut locally in Accra, Kumasi and Takoradi. Its strengths are heat resistance and the fact that templating, cutting and fitting are done by local fabricators who can come back for adjustments. The limitations are porosity in lighter colours, edge chipping, weight (base units and floors must carry it), and slab-to-slab variation, which matters on multi-unit developments where every kitchen should match.

## Quartz: the consistent choice

Engineered quartz offers a uniform colour and a non-porous surface that shrugs off oil and tomato stains. Its weak point is heat: the resin binder can discolour or crack under a pan straight from a gas flame. For a household that cooks hard, quartz works with disciplined use of trivets, or it can be used on the main runs with a stainless or sintered insert beside the hob. Quartz is usually imported as slabs or as pre-cut worktops with the kitchen.

## Sintered stone: the premium option

Sintered stone and large-format porcelain worktops combine heat, stain and scratch resistance and are the usual choice for the Premium tier. They are thinner and lighter than granite for the same span, support flush-mounted hobs and sinks, and offer marble and stone looks without the porosity. The caveat is fabrication: cutting, mitred edges and cut-outs need the right tools and experience, so they are best supplied pre-cut to the kitchen drawings, with careful crating for shipment and a trained installer on site.

## Local vs import: which route for which material

| Material | Usual route for Ghana | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Granite | Buy and cut locally | Stock available; templated after cabinets are fitted; heavy to ship |
| Quartz | Import pre-cut with the kitchen, or import slabs for local cutting | Colour consistency; pre-cut to drawings reduces site work |
| Sintered stone | Import pre-cut with the kitchen | Fabrication skill and tooling |
| Solid surface | Import pre-formed or fabricate locally where a skilled shop exists | Seamless joints need trained hands |
| Laminate | Import with the kitchen or source locally | Low cost; check MR substrate |

The decision usually comes down to two things: whether every kitchen on the project must match, and whether the cabinets are being imported anyway. If the cabinets are coming in a container, pre-cut quartz or sintered tops add little to freight and arrive matched to the cabinet drawings. If the cabinets are built locally, a locally cut granite top templated on site is the lower-risk path.

## Templating, tolerances and installation

Pre-cut worktops only work when the cabinets are installed level and to drawing. The sequence that avoids problems:

1. Cabinets installed, levelled and fixed to the wall.
2. Site check of the actual cabinet run length, wall straightness and the hob and sink cut-out positions against the worktop drawing.
3. Worktop pieces laid dry, joints checked, then fixed and jointed with the correct adhesive.
4. Upstands or splashbacks fitted last.

Allow for Ghanaian block walls that are rarely straight: a 20–30 mm overhang at the wall and a scribed upstand hide the gap. Keep joints away from the sink and hob cut-outs, and support the worktop beside a freestanding cooker gap.

## Caring for the surface

- Granite: re-seal every one to two years; wipe oil promptly on light colours.
- Quartz: trivets for hot pans; avoid bleach soaking.
- Sintered stone: almost no care; avoid dropping heavy items on edges.
- Laminate: wipe water at joints; never leave a wet cloth on a joint.

## Sinks, hobs and the worktop together

The worktop decision is linked to the sink and hob. An undermount sink needs a non-porous worktop with a polished cut-out, so it suits quartz and sintered stone and is harder to do well in laminate. A top-mount (drop-in) sink works with any material and hides the cut-out edge, which is the safer choice with granite cut on site. Gas hobs put flame close to the surface edge; keep a 50 mm margin of worktop around the hob cut-out and, with quartz, consider a stainless strip or a sintered insert at the hob. Confirm the sink and hob models, with their cut-out templates, before the worktop is cut anywhere.

## Back kitchens and outdoor cooking areas

Many Ghanaian houses have a back kitchen for heavy cooking. There is no need to match the show kitchen's worktop there: a stainless steel top on a steel or masonry base, or a locally cut dark granite, takes charcoal heat, big pots and scrubbing without complaint. Spend the money on the main kitchen worktop that guests see and keep the back kitchen practical.

## FAQ

**Is quartz a bad idea if we cook with heavy pots on gas?**
Not if trivets are used consistently. If that is unrealistic for the household, choose granite or sintered stone.

**Can a worktop be shipped already cut for the sink and hob?**
Yes, when the sink and hob models are confirmed before production. Changing appliances after cutting is expensive.

**Which worktop is best for a rental apartment?**
Quartz or a good laminate on MR board for the Functional tier; quartz or sintered stone for corporate lets.

**How thick should the worktop be?**
Common choices are 20 mm for quartz and granite with a built-up edge, and 12 mm for sintered stone. The thicknesses we supply are confirmed on your quote.
